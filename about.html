How Springboard works

Turn any stimulus (an image, a short video, an article or a link) into a ready-to-run lesson launch for any subject and year level, exported as an editable Wesley PowerPoint.

← Back to Springboard

The idea

You bring the spark (a picture, a clip, a news article), and a couple of minutes later you have a Wesley PowerPoint that gets students thinking hard in the first minute of the lesson. You choose the kind of thinking you want them to do; Springboard works out how to open with it, writes the slides and the teacher script, and hands the whole thing over for you to edit. It works across the school, from Prep to VCE.

The slides

Three slides face the class. The rest of the deck is for you, and PowerPoint skips those when you present.

Slide 1

Ignite

A provocation built from your stimulus: one question worth arguing about, the picture itself, and the learning intention in small type underneath.

Slide 2

Think

A real Harvard thinking routine, its actual moves rewritten for your stimulus, beside the picture or the article summary students are working from.

Slide 3

Reflect

A short revisit at the end of the lesson that sends students back to the opening question and asks them to notice how they thought, not only what they concluded. You can turn it off.

Teacher only

The rest

A "read this first" opener, an optional content advisory for sensitive themes, and three follow-up lesson ideas behind a slide that explains what they are. Hidden, so students never see them.

Every field is editable in Springboard before you download, any single part can be rewritten on its own ("New options"), and the teacher script (how to run it, timing, a prompt for students who are stuck, one for students who need pushing, curriculum links) travels in the PowerPoint's Notes pane. Pick the Zoom In routine over a photo and you also get the reveal slides, cropped the way you set them.

The flow

  1. Choose a thinking move. Start on the Understanding Map and pick what you want students doing: describing what's there, reasoning with evidence, considering other viewpoints, and so on. Everything after this serves that choice.
  2. Add your stimulus. Drop in images or short videos, paste or upload an article or PDF, or paste a link (a news story, a YouTube clip, a social post). A link comes back as a QR code on the slide too.
  3. Set the class. Curriculum, subject and year level. Where Springboard knows your curriculum it offers the real focus points to tick. You can add a learning focus in your own words, and either write the learning intention yourself or have Springboard write one on the spot and try another until it fits.
  4. Pick the routine. Springboard reads your stimulus and suggests the routines for your thinking move that suit it and the age group, with a line on why each one fits. Take one, or browse the rest.
  5. Make the lesson. Read it, change any wording, ask for new options on a single part, or add the content-advisory slide if a theme needs a gentle heads-up.
  6. Download. Save the Wesley PowerPoint and teach from it. It's an ordinary .pptx, so you can keep editing it in PowerPoint like any other deck.

The thinking beneath

Springboard doesn't just paraphrase your stimulus. Behind the scenes it writes every lesson against a small set of well-evidenced teaching ideas, so the output is designed to teach, not just to fill slides.

Made to Stick (Chip & Dan Heath)

Made to Stick is a book about why some ideas are remembered and repeated while better ideas are forgotten. The authors studied the ideas that survive (urban myths, the best teachers, the best campaigns) and found six things they have in common. The initials spell SUCCESs, and Springboard writes every lesson against them. Nothing for you to switch on.

The book's villain is the Curse of Knowledge: once you know something, you can't remember what not knowing felt like, so you explain it at the wrong altitude. It's the reason a clear explanation can land on a class as noise. Springboard is instructed to write for someone meeting the topic cold.

  • Simple means one core idea, said in a line students could repeat at the lockers. Not dumbed down, decided: you cut good material so the important thing survives.
  • Unexpected means opening a gap they want closed. Break what they assume, get every student to commit to a guess out loud or on paper, then reveal. Curiosity comes from a gap in their knowledge they can feel.
  • Concrete means things you could point at: a named person, a real place, a date, an object in the picture. Abstractions are a luxury of people who already understand.
  • Credible means they can check it themselves rather than take your word for it. Best is a "see for yourself" test; next best is a vivid detail, or a number scaled to something they can picture.
  • Emotional means a reason to care, and people care about one person, not a statistic. It also means identity: what would someone like me do here?
  • Story means the task is shaped so students mentally rehearse doing it: overcoming a challenge, connecting people who disagree, or cracking something open.

These run right through the deck, from the opening question to the reflection, and they have to land on the slides students actually read, not only in your notes.

Harvard Project Zero thinking routines

A thinking routine is a short, repeatable set of moves that makes thinking visible: See, Think, Wonder asks students to separate what they can actually see from what they reckon it means and what it makes them curious about. Project Zero is the Harvard research group that developed them, and they're designed to be used often enough to become a habit, not as a one-off activity.

Springboard groups them by the thinking move you chose on the Understanding Map, suggests the ones that suit your stimulus and year level, and then rewrites that routine's real steps for your stimulus rather than inventing its own. The routine's proper name and its source stay on the slide and in your notes, so students meet the same routine the same way each time.

Metacognition in the Reflect (thinking about thinking)

Metacognition simply means thinking about your own thinking: planning how you'll tackle something, noticing while you work whether it's going well, then judging afterwards what to keep doing. Teach it explicitly and students get better at steering themselves, which is one of the better-evidenced things a teacher can spend time on.

So the Reflect slide doesn't only revisit what students concluded, it asks how they got there. Every lesson closes with at least one forward-looking prompt (what I'd do differently, or which strategy I'll reuse next time), tied to that lesson rather than a generic "how did it go".

  • Task: did my plan or approach work today, and how do I know?
  • Strategy: which strategy helped most, and what would help me use it better?
  • Self: what did I learn about myself as a learner?

The teacher notes carry the full question bank and a "least help first" way to run it, so the reflection feeds straight into planning the next lesson.

Your curriculum, quoted rather than guessed

Curriculum wording is the thing an AI is most tempted to invent, so Springboard doesn't let it. When it recognises your subject and year, it loads the real document on the server and hands the relevant extract to the lesson writer, which is why the curriculum links quote actual phrases and codes.

  • Australian Curriculum v9 (Foundation to Year 10): the content descriptions and achievement standard for that subject at that year level, with the real AC9 codes.
  • VCE study designs (Years 11 and 12): the official VCAA study design for your study, narrowed to Units 1–2 for Year 11 or Units 3–4 for Year 12, with its Areas of Study and outcomes.

Where a curriculum is loaded you can tick the specific points this lesson targets, and the whole lesson is built around those instead of the subject in general. Teaching something outside those two, or in your own words? Type your learning focus in plain English and it works from that, without inventing codes.

Pitched to the year level

The wording, the routine list and the depth all shift with the year level, across four bands: very concrete and spoken for the early years (Prep to Year 2), concrete and reason-giving for the junior years (3 to 6), analytical and real-world for the middle years (7 to 9), and critical, ethical and conceptual for senior students (10 to 12). A Prep deck and a Year 6 deck are written differently, not just labelled differently.

Behind the scenes

Lessons are written by an AI model. The key that runs it lives on a secure Wesley backend, not in the app and not on your device, so you never enter a key, and nothing is stored on your computer. Your stimulus text or image is sent to the model only to generate the lesson.
